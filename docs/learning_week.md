# A week worth returning to — worked teaching design

> September 22, 2026. A design specimen for the wider tutor commission, not a
> timetable, learner assignment or reusable lesson script. Replaces the abstract
> curriculum discussion in [comprehension_plan.md](comprehension_plan.md).
> No encounters below have happened. These printed examples cannot later count as
> unfamiliar listening material. Live preparation uses fresh state and fresh content.

## The bet

Andrew should have two reasons to return: something interesting awaits, and Tamil
that previously went past him is becoming intelligible. Either can carry a particular
day. Across encounters, the teacher must deliver both. A week is a useful design
scale because a single charming exchange cannot show continuity; it is not a quota.

Start from the September 21 evidence: Andrew understood possession and location in
supported text and built a question from a nearby model. We have not established
that he hears those distinctions. The next useful expansion is a change in a
situation: where something **was**, where it **is**, and why that matters now.
This connects to the ignored July question about *irundhuchu*, without treating an
old obstacle as a mandatory repair.

The thread has an end. Once a changed account makes sense, leave the key behind;
do not manufacture a month of missing possessions. Future threads can concern
arrivals, an altered plan, a meal, or something Andrew actually wants to understand.

## First encounter: a gift, then something changes

The gift need not foreshadow the exercise. One possible opening:

> English already has a little Tamil tucked away in its kitchen. *Curry* came
> from Tamil *kari*. You can meet a word in English for years before discovering
> which language it travelled from. Today Tamil gets to be the familiar thing
> hiding inside the unfamiliar one.

Oxford records the borrowing in the late sixteenth century. That supports the
word connection, not a sweeping account of modern Tamil food names or a made-up
story about who carried it. [Source: Oxford Learner's Dictionaries](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/us/definition/english/curry_1).
If this is already familiar, preparation chooses another sourced connection.
If it sparks a question, that conversation can be the whole encounter. No later
recall demand is attached to the gift.

For the teaching, supply a reason to listen: somebody is trying to leave and has
received an out-of-date answer about the key. Prepare a short playable clip before
the chat. Give the misunderstanding a small payoff: the confidently accused key-holder
has already handed it over. Let the innocent person enjoy the correction; Andrew can
enjoy it without answering. No elaborate mystery is needed. An explanatory specimen:

> Key enkitta irundhuchu. Ippo Mama kitta irukku.

“The key was with me. Now Mama has it.” This gives Andrew a changed state to follow,
not another object to substitute. First contact with a new form is teaching: let
him hear the sound, supply its meaning generously, and replay. Do not turn the first
hearing into a cold test. If a familiar part is unclear, explain that too.

Make the contrast audible and visible: *enkitta irukku* / *enkitta irundhuchu*;
then return to the whole utterance. The practical payoff is that the earlier
holder no longer answers “who should I ask?” Avoid teaching all past-tense endings
because one sentence opened the door; pursue them if Andrew's curiosity does.

A reply such as “so ask Mama” demonstrates the meaning more directly than asking
for an English word at every step. If prompted immediately after the explanation,
it is supported understanding. Enjoy it without promoting it to independent listening.

Close with the specific gain, contingent on what actually happened: “The sentence
can now tell you who had it before, as well as who has it now.” Leave the short
clip available for replay, and choose one existing longer artifact for the separate
ear block. Give its particular reason: “this makes that ending easier to hear” or
“this is the story you enjoyed,” only when the content or testimony supports that.
A pleasurable unrelated replay is welcome. Newest does not mean familiar or heard.

## A later encounter: follow the interest, widen the meaning

Open with another worthwhile gift, not “let's see what you remember.” If Andrew
asks why a form changes, follow the question. A satisfying explanation can itself
be new ground. No unanswered quiz should trail behind it.

One continuation gives the earlier distinction another job: the scooter was at home;
now it is at the office. Someone's earlier offer of a lift no longer works. Supply
any unfamiliar surrounding language and let the known contrast unlock why the plan
changes. That is accumulation: an earlier distinction explains a new consequence.

If a different interest leads, move from possession to an everyday expectation:
someone is being included in a meal, and whether they have eaten changes the plan.
For illustration, *Innum saappidala* means “haven't eaten yet” in the supplied
context. Teach the whole useful message, including the understood person, rather
than asking him to decode an unknown verb unaided.

The teacher's useful choice is whether to deepen the earlier past/present contrast
or introduce this expectation. Current understanding and curiosity decide; “day
two” does not authorize more intake. Check the existing inventory and calibration
before choosing. The printed examples are alternative design material, not a list
to cover.

The household can carry the situation, but belonging to a fictional cast is not
the payoff. If it feels contrived, use an ordinary exchange about a meal or a real
fragment Andrew volunteers. Do not invent testimony from his wife or relatives.
If he is interested in a different subject, develop the same conversational
ability there. The teacher supplies that alternative without presenting a menu.

## A delayed return: give the language a new job

After some intervening contact, offer a fresh spoken exchange using previously
taught language. Supply the situation but withhold the transcript initially.
Make understanding change a decision: who can help now, whether to wait, or
whether an invitation still makes sense. One natural response is enough to learn
something about the teaching; it need not become a battery of questions.

For a past/present revisit, change who has the object and which state is mentioned
first. Keep voice and speed familiar initially. The earlier story must not supply
the answer, and one recognizable noun must not solve the whole question. If the
response is ambiguous, ask what he meant before logging an outcome.

If it lands, later vary a speaker or add a short surrounding turn. If it does not,
replay, reveal, explain and enjoy the now-understandable exchange. That is a useful
lesson too; record the support rather than calling it a failed week. Separate
understanding the message from identifying the particular form responsible: a
correct gist is not automatic evidence that every word was recognized.

There is room for participation: asking who has something, correcting an assumption,
accepting an invitation. Offer it when the exchange invites a reply. Andrew need
not perform to earn the next interesting thing.

## The spaces between, and a return after a gap

Standing audio remains available independently of chat. The teacher selects one
useful replay at a close; it does not create another commissioning obligation or
displace the existing ear-block agreement. Short lesson clips are optional returns,
not a new listening schedule. A phone message can be a complete tiny pleasure,
with no request for a response. Silence does not prove it failed.

If Andrew asks “what does that ending do?” on the phone, answer that question in
its original context. Suspend the pending exercise. A subsequent chat must not
mark the question wrong, advance an exercise as if answered, or re-ask it by default.

After a gap, Anna brings the current situation in a sentence and something worth
receiving. There is no plot homework. Teach afresh where useful, using what was
retained as a convenience rather than collecting evidence of forgetting. Reopen
the thread only if it still has life; a finished or stale story can stay finished.

## How this becomes cumulative without another syllabus machine

Keep a concise teacher handoff in the existing debrief: what became understandable,
the medium and support, the live question, a selected replay, and a promising next
move. The next move is provisional. Do not add a weekly form or learner checklist.

At a natural thread ending, preparation asks what conversation has become possible
and what useful ground has been neglected. Use the existing inventory to inspect
that judgment. If several encounters only rename objects, expand the meaning or
retire the thread. If everything is new and nothing becomes familiar, consolidate.
Preserve unfinished curiosity as well as trouble: memory should remember what he
wants to find out, not only what he missed.

Illustrative longer direction: locating things → following changes → understanding
why a plan changed → contributing to the revised plan. This is a progression of
conversation, not a dated promise of mastery or a fixed vocabulary sequence.
